# Unicorn-Companies-Analysis
For this project, we analyze a dataset of global unicorn ompanies that have achieved a valuation of $1 billion or more. The main goal was to uncover patterns around when these companies were founded and how long it took them to reach unicorn status.

We began by loading the data and conducting an initial inspection of its shape, data types, and first few records. The dataset comprises 1,074 entries across 10 features, covering details like company valuation, market sector, location, and investors. First step was converting the date joined column from a  string object into a datetime format, which allowed us to pull out specific attributes like years, months, and week numbers.  

Our exploratory analysis yielded several key findings. Looking at the distribution of founding years, we found that most unicorns in the dataset originated between 2012 and 2018, with 2015 marking the single highest peak at 155 companies. We then computed a new metric "years needed to join" by subtracting the founding year from the year unicorn status was achieved. I examined specific year 2020. I was interested how fast companies become unicorns during covid-time. Interestingly, for companies founded in 2020, growth was remarkably fast: 21 of the 25 startups became unicorns within their very first year. A boxplot across months was used to map out these growth windows further.We isolated the 2020 cohort and extracted ISO week numbers to see if additions clustered at specific points in the year. Plotting these weekly counts on a bar graph highlighted clear surges during the final weeks of the year, particularly around weeks 49 and 50.

Lastly, i tried to get info about how much years companies needed to become unicorn based on their found year. As our study found, companies founded more recently are becoming unicorn companies much faster than other companies.

In the extended file, I mainly concentrated on the data cleaning process. I transformed the Valuation column from string to integer by removing the string characters ('B$') and updating the data type. Then, I used boolean masking to identify any missing values (NaN/Null). I found a single missing value in the Select Investors column and 16 missing values in the City column, with no missing values in any other columns. 

I also identified the locations of these missing values; twelve of the 17 rows with missing data belong to companies from Singapore.  The next step was finding an appropriate way to handle the missing values. My options were removing columns, removing rows, or using a back-fill method. Removing the columns with missing values reduced the total dataset size by roughly 5,000 cells out of 15,000—a significant 33% loss of data. The back-fill method would not work effectively here, as imputing random city names would introduce misleading information. Therefore, removing the affected rows proved to be the best option, resulting in only about 200 cells removed. 

 I created a scenario where an investor was interested in identifying unicorn companies in the Hardware industry across Beijing, San Francisco, and London, as well as companies in the Artificial Intelligence industry in London. Using a boolean mask, I filtered the dataset based on these exact criteria.  Finally, I calculated the sum of all company valuations for each country and sorted the results in descending order. The sorted data showed that the four countries with the highest total company valuations are the United States, China, India, and the United Kingdom. 
 
I considered another scenario where an investor specified excluding these top four countries because they represent market outliers. Using a boolean mask with the NOT (~) operator, I excluded these countries and generated a bar plot and dynamic map highlighting the top 20 non-big-4 countries with the highest total company valuations.
